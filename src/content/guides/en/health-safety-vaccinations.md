San Blas is remote in a way that changes the calculation on ordinary things. A blister, a forgotten prescription or an unprotected shoulder is a minor problem in a city and a genuine one on an island several hours from the nearest clinic. None of this makes the trip risky — it makes preparation worth twenty minutes of your time.

**This page is general travel guidance, not medical advice.** Vaccination recommendations and health-entry rules change, so confirm current requirements with your doctor, a travel health clinic, or Panama's official government and embassy sources before you travel.

## Before you leave home

- **Ask about vaccinations for Panama and the Guna Yala region.** Recommendations — yellow fever among them — depend on your medical history, your itinerary and the country you are arriving from, so a generic internet checklist is not a substitute for a clinic's advice on your specific case.
- **Take out travel insurance.** A remote archipelago hours from a hospital is precisely the scenario insurance exists for, and it costs a fraction of an evacuation.
- **Bring every medication you take**, for the whole trip plus a margin. There are no pharmacies anywhere in the islands and nothing can be sent out to you.

## What "remote" means once you are there

There are no hospitals or clinics on the islands. Medical care is in Panama City, hours away by the same boat-and-4x4 route you arrived on, and a serious situation means evacuation rather than a local visit. That is one reason reputable operators build genuine emergencies into their cancellation and refund policies, and a good reason to mention any significant condition when you book rather than on the morning of departure.

## The three things that actually go wrong

- **Sun.** This is by far the most common real problem. Caribbean sun reflects off white sand and flat water, so exposure adds up much faster than people expect. Reef-safe sunscreen, a hat, a rash guard for snorkelling and steady water intake matter more here than on almost any other trip.
- **Motion sickness.** The 4x4 road to Carti is a long series of tight mountain curves, and the boat crossing follows it. If you are prone to either, medicate **before** departure, not once symptoms start.
- **Dehydration.** Long day, strong sun, salt water. Drink more than you think you need, and bring water rather than assuming it is on sale on a cay.

Evenings on an overnight stay are also when insect repellent earns its place in the bag.

## Food, water and dietary needs

Meals are prepared for visitors as part of every tour, and the cooking leans on fresh fish and local staples. If you have a serious allergy or a strict dietary requirement, flag it when you book rather than on the island: options are limited by what a small kitchen can carry, and bringing a backup snack is sensible either way.

## Who should think twice

Operators are consistently direct about this: San Blas is **not recommended** for pregnant travelers, for people with back or hip conditions, or for anyone with a serious medical condition that sun, boat travel or distance from care could aggravate. The trip is also **not wheelchair accessible** — it involves boarding boats from the water's edge and walking on soft sand.

## On the water

Life jackets are mandatory at all times for adults and children alike, and the boats are handled by experienced local Guna captains who read these waters daily. Crime is not a meaningful feature of a San Blas trip; ordinary sense about valuables covers the realistic risk far better than anything more exotic.

## Frequently asked questions

### What vaccinations do I need for San Blas?

There is no single answer that fits every traveler, which is why we do not publish a list. Requirements and recommendations vary with your history, your route and your country of departure — check with your doctor or a travel health clinic, and with Panama's official sources, close to your travel date.

### Is there a doctor or a hospital in San Blas?

No. There are no hospitals, clinics or pharmacies on the islands. Medical care means returning to Panama City, several hours away by boat and road, which is why travel insurance and bringing your own medication matter here more than usual.

### Is San Blas safe for tourists?

Yes, in the ordinary sense. Crime is low, boats are crewed by experienced local captains, and life jackets are compulsory for everyone on board. The real risks are environmental — sun, dehydration and motion sickness — rather than criminal.

### Do I need travel insurance?

It is strongly advisable. The distance between the islands and full medical facilities is exactly the kind of exposure insurance is designed to cover, and evacuation from a remote archipelago is not cheap.

### Is the trip suitable if I am pregnant or have back problems?

It is not recommended. Long road curves, boat boarding and hours of sun are difficult combinations for pregnancy and for back or hip conditions, and medical help is hours away. Anyone in either category should take medical advice before booking.

### How do I avoid getting sick on the road to Carti?

Take motion sickness medication before you set off, not after symptoms appear — it is much less effective once nausea has started. Sitting forward in the vehicle and looking at the road ahead helps, and the curves ease once you reach the coast.

### Is the food and water safe?

Meals are cooked for visitors as part of the tour and are a normal part of the trip. Carry your own drinking water for the day, mention allergies and dietary restrictions at the time of booking, and pack a backup snack if you are hard to cater for.
