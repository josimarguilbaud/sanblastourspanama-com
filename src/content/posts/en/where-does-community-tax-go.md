**The entry tax — $22 for foreigners, $7 for Panamanian residents — is charged by the Guna authorities, not by Panama's government or by your tour operator. It is one of the ways the Guna General Congress funds a self-government that its own law requires to be self-sustaining, and research on the comarca links that income to the Congress's staff, community equipment and patrols of the territory.**

## Who charges it, and why not the national government

Guna Yala is a comarca: a territory whose people govern themselves through their own congresses, recognized by Panamanian law since 1953. The highest political authority is the Guna General Congress, and the territory's rules are written in its own Fundamental Law. Two of its articles explain where the tax fits:

- **Article 60** says the general congresses sustain themselves economically through an autonomous, self-managed system. In other words, Guna government is expected to fund itself rather than live on transfers from Panama City.
- **Article 59** says every for-profit activity carried out in Guna Yala pays a contribution to the General Congress, in proportion to its economic capacity.

The visitor's entry tax is the most visible piece of that system. It is collected at the checkpoint on the road into the territory, it is charged per person, and the operator who drives you there keeps none of it. The text of the law is published by the Congress itself on its [official website](https://www.gunayala.org.pa/Ley%20Fundamenta.Gunayala..pdf).

## What the money pays for

There is no public, itemized account of what each visitor's $22 buys, and we have not found one. What exists is research. The most detailed study we know of is by anthropologist Mònica Martínez Mauri, published in the geography journal Norois in 2018 under the title [Why pay to enter Gunayala?](https://journals.openedition.org/norois/6609) (in Spanish). Based on fieldwork and the Congress's own rules, she describes how these charges are used:

- At the level of the **General Congress**, they help pay the salaries of its technical staff and the costs of running a government that meets in a different community each session.
- At the level of the **communities**, the fees they collect themselves go into shared equipment such as boats and power generators.
- The same revenue finances **patrols of the territory's river basins**, the mainland forests the islands depend on for water and farmland.

She also cites an earlier study estimating that tourism charges made up **27.4 %** of the Congress's total income at the time. Treat that as a historical snapshot rather than today's figure; it does show that visitor fees are not a symbolic amount for Guna self-government.

On oversight, the Fundamental Law gives the General Congress the duty to supervise Gunayala's funds from any internal or external source, including what the national budget assigns to development programmes (Article 22).

## Who collects what inside the territory

The entry tax is not the only charge, and it helps to see the layers side by side. The table summarizes the structure Martínez Mauri documented; exact amounts change over time.

| Level | What is charged | Who collects it | What it supports |
|---|---|---|---|
| The comarca | Entry tax per visitor: $22 foreigners, $7 residents | The Guna General Congress, at the road checkpoint | The Congress's work, staff and territorial patrols |
| Each community | Small landing fees on some islands and ports | That community's local congress | Boats, generators and local upkeep |
| Sailboats and yachts | A cruising fee for time in Guna waters | The General Congress | The same comarca-wide functions |
| Guna tourism businesses | A contribution to the Congress (Article 59) | The General Congress | The same comarca-wide functions |

When Martínez Mauri studied it in the mid-2010s, the fee at the checkpoint on the road down from El Llano was $20 for foreigners. Today it is $22.

## A tax that is also a statement of autonomy

For the Guna, the right to charge visitors at the door is not a technicality. Martínez Mauri describes the ability to collect an entry tax as an essential attribute of Guna territorial autonomy, and a clear way of asserting sovereignty over their land. The history behind that position runs through the 1925 Guna Revolution, told in our [history of San Blas](/guides/history-of-san-blas), and through the laws that later recognized the comarca, explained in [the Guna General Congress explained](/blog/guna-general-congress-explained).

That is also why the tax has been politically contested. In 2016, according to the same study, Panama's then Minister of Government publicly called the Guna charges illegal, arguing that collecting them was a function of the state; the General Congress responded by declaring him persona non grata. The fee is still collected today. For visitors, the takeaway is simple: paying it is recognizing whose territory you are entering.

## How it fits with the limits on tourism

The same Fundamental Law that funds the Congress also shapes the kind of tourism you find. Article 52 allows the General Congress to limit both the number of tourist sites and the number of visitors, in favour of selective, ecological tourism. Article 53 reserves all tourism activity in the comarca to the Guna, and Article 54 requires each Guna tourism business to hold a written authorization, renewed every two years.

Martínez Mauri notes that, in practice, the Congress has regulated through fees and permits rather than through visitor quotas. The result is what you see on the cays: small, family-run cabins and boats, no foreign hotel chains, and an entry fee that goes to the territory rather than to a national tourism board. ## Paying it: the practical details

- **Amount:** $22 per foreign visitor, $7 for Panamanian residents who show a passport or residency card. Children under 10 are exempt.
- **When and where:** on the day you travel, at the comarca checkpoint on the road in, before you reach the port of Cartí.
- **How:** in cash, ideally counted out per person in small bills. On our tours the driver can also take it by card, as our [prices page](/prices) explains.
- **Not in any tour price:** on our tours or anyone else's, because it is not the operator's money.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who receives the San Blas entry tax?

The Guna authorities. The tax is collected for the Guna General Congress at the comarca checkpoint, and the tour operator keeps none of it. It is not a fee of Panama's national government or of its tourism authority.

### What is the Guna Yala tax used for?

There is no public itemized breakdown. Research on the comarca describes these charges funding the General Congress's staff and operations, patrols of the territory's river basins and, at community level, shared equipment such as boats and generators.

### Is the Guna Yala entry tax legal?

Panamanian law has recognized the Guna General Congress and its jurisdiction since 1953, and the Guna's own Fundamental Law provides for contributions to the Congress. National officials have contested the charges in the past, notably in 2016, but the tax continues to be collected at the entry checkpoint.

### Why is the tax not included in the tour price?

Because it is not the operator's money. It is a charge of the Guna authorities, paid by each visitor at the checkpoint, so every tour quotes its price without it.

### Do children pay the Guna Yala tax?

Children under 10 are exempt. From age 10, foreigners pay $22 and Panamanian residents $7.
