**No. Nobody from outside the Guna people can buy, own or permanently lease an island in San Blas. The archipelago is the comarca of Guna Yala, where every island, islet and cay is the collective property of the Guna. Their Fundamental Law declares that land inalienable, and Panamanian law has barred outsiders from acquiring it since 1953.**

## Who owns the islands of San Blas?

The Guna people, collectively. San Blas is not a province with a land registry open to buyers; it is [Guna Yala](/guna-yala), an Indigenous territory with its own congresses and its own law. The [Fundamental Law of Gunayala](https://www.gunayala.org.pa/Ley%20Fundamenta.Gunayala..pdf), approved by the Guna General Congresses in September 2013 at Mirya Ubgigandub, describes the territory in its article 4 as a mainland and island portion that takes in every island, islet, cay and reef, plus the sea along the coast. Article 34 then declares those lands the collective property of the Guna people, to be acquired, worked and enjoyed according to Guna norms and practices.

By Guna custom, a particular cay, and above all its coconut palms, is usually tended by a family or a community. That is why the boatman can tell you whose island you are standing on. But it is a right of use within the Guna system, not a title deed, and it cannot be sold to an outsider.

Land is scarcer than it looks. Of the archipelago's many islands ([we count them here](/blog/how-many-islands-does-san-blas-have)), only about 49 or 50 communities are inhabited, the 2023 census counted 32,016 people in the whole comarca, and some communities are so crowded that they are moving to the mainland, as we explain in [are the San Blas islands sinking?](/blog/are-san-blas-islands-sinking).

## What the law says, rule by rule

The ban is written into three layers of law: Panamanian statutes, the Constitution and the Guna's own Fundamental Law.

| Rule | Where it comes from | What it means for a buyer |
|---|---|---|
| The comarca exists as a legal territory | Law 2 of 1938 created the Comarca de San Blas | The islands sit inside a special Indigenous territory, not ordinary private land |
| No land goes to non-members unless two Guna congresses approve | Law 16 of 1953, article 21 | Being a foreigner or a Panamanian is irrelevant; what matters is belonging to the community |
| Land abandoned for more than five years returns to the reserve | Law 16 of 1953, article 22 | Even land once granted does not stay granted indefinitely |
| Indigenous lands are held collectively and private appropriation is banned | Panama's Constitution | The national framework points the same way as the Guna one |
| Land is imprescriptible, unseizable and inalienable, and cannot be sold or leased permanently under any title | Fundamental Law, article 33 | No sale, no permanent lease, no title by long occupation; earlier private claims are declared null |
| Land is the collective property of the Guna people | Fundamental Law, article 34 | Families use islands; they do not own them as saleable property |
| The General Congress may authorise the use of a defined plot by agreement, for up to 10 years, subject to review | Fundamental Law, article 35 | The only route to legal use runs through the congress, never through a private seller |
| All tourism is reserved to the Guna, with written authorisation renewed every two years | Fundamental Law, articles 53 and 54 | An outsider cannot own a hotel or cabin business here |
| Tourism that breaks those rules is void, and its assets can be confiscated | Fundamental Law, article 55 | Money invested in a non-compliant project has no protection |

Law 16 of 1953, which organised the comarca, was published in Panama's Official Gazette No. 12042 on 7 April 1953 ([record on Justia](https://panama.justia.com/federales/leyes/16-de-1953-apr-7-1953/gdoc/)). Its article 21, as transcribed by the legal scholar Carlos Durand Alcántara in the journal [Alegatos](https://alegatos.azc.uam.mx/index.php/ra/article/view/1041/1018), says that land inside the Indigenous reserves will not be granted to anyone who is not part of the community unless two different Guna congresses approve the request. The same transcription quotes Panama's Constitution guaranteeing Indigenous communities the collective ownership of their land and prohibiting its private appropriation.

The Fundamental Law goes further. Where Law 16 left a narrow door open with congressional approval, article 33 closes it: no sale or permanent lease under any title, and earlier private claims are null. How the congress that enforces this works is explained in our [guide to the Guna General Congress](/blog/guna-general-congress-explained).

## So what are the "island for sale in San Blas" listings?

They exist, usually with a picture-perfect cay and a handful of palms. They fall into three groups, and none gives you an island.

**1. Islands that are not in Guna Yala at all.** Panama has other island groups, such as Bocas del Toro on the Caribbean and Las Perlas on the Pacific, where most land falls outside comarca law. Sellers borrow the San Blas name because it is the famous one. Even outside the comarca, island deals deserve caution: in June 2024 [La Prensa](https://www.prensa.com/judiciales/ministerio-publico-investiga-venta-irregular-de-tierras-en-islas-del-archipielago-de-las-perlas/) reported that prosecutors were investigating the irregular sale of state land on two islands in Las Perlas, a case involving a US businessman and municipal officials.

**2. "Rights" bought from a Guna family.** Someone offers to transfer their family's use of a cay in exchange for money. Under article 33 that transfer is not possible under any title, and under article 35 the only legal way to use a plot is an agreement authorised by the General Congress, limited to ten years. A family cannot sign the territory away on its own, however sincere the handshake.

**3. A stake in a tourism business.** The pitch is a partnership: you finance cabins, a Guna family runs them. Articles 53 to 55 reserve tourism to the Guna, require the congress's written authorisation and allow non-compliant operations to be voided and their assets confiscated. A foreign partner has nothing to enforce.

In short: any money you pay buys no title, and the comarca can undo the arrangement.

## Why the Guna hold this line

Control over land is the foundation of Guna autonomy, which was fought for in the 1925 Dule Revolution and then written into law step by step, as our [history of San Blas](/guides/history-of-san-blas) tells. Selling islands would hollow that out one cay at a time. The result is visible from any boat: no resorts, no foreign-owned hotels, no gated private islands, and every cabin and boat in Guna hands, as our [eco-tourism guide](/guides/eco-tourism) explains.

## What you can do instead

If the dream is an island to yourself, there are legal ways to get close:

- **Spend the night.** Guna-run cabins on small islands give you the island after the day boats leave. Our [overnight stays guide](/guides/overnight-stays) compares the options, and current rates live on the [prices page](/prices).
- **Go private.** A private boat lets you choose quieter cays and stay longer, always as a guest of whoever looks after them.
- **Stay longer, legally.** Foreigners must present themselves to the saglas of the communities they visit and to migration authorities (article 56). Long stays are arranged, not bought.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can foreigners buy land in Guna Yala?

No. The Fundamental Law of Gunayala makes the land inalienable and collective, and Law 16 of 1953 already barred granting it to outsiders without the approval of two Guna congresses.

### Can a Panamanian who is not Guna buy an island in San Blas?

No. The rule is about belonging to the Guna community, not nationality, and the Fundamental Law bans any sale or permanent lease whoever the buyer is.

### Can I lease an island long term?

Not privately. The only legal route is an agreement authorised by the Guna General Congress for the use of a defined plot, for no more than ten years and subject to review. A lease signed with a family alone has no standing.

### Who owns the uninhabited islands?

The Guna people collectively. In practice a family or a community usually tends each cay and its coconut palms.

### Can I open a hotel or a cabin business in San Blas?

Not as an outsider. All tourism in the comarca is reserved to the Guna, and every operation needs the General Congress's written authorisation, renewed every two years. Projects that break those rules can be voided and their assets confiscated.
