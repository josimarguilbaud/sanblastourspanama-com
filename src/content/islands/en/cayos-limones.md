The Limones Cays are not one island. They are a cluster of small uninhabited islets sitting close enough together that a boat can move between two or three of them inside a single afternoon — tiny circles of white sand ringed by palms, in water so clear you can read the bottom from the boat before anyone jumps in.

## Island-hopping the way it is supposed to work

Most San Blas stops involve a real crossing between them. Limones does not: swim off one cay, climb back aboard, ride five minutes, swim off the next. That geography is why premium itineraries favour this group — it fits several genuinely different beaches into a stretch of the day that elsewhere would buy you one.

## Why the water looks like that

Clear water is not luck here. There is no river mouth nearby pushing sediment out, no settlement on the cays, and the sand is pale enough to bounce light back up through the shallows. The practical effect is that snorkeling is rewarding even in very shallow water close to shore, and that the difference between waist-deep and chest-deep is visible from the surface.

## Where Limones appears on an itinerary

Two places. The VIP day tour reaches this group as part of its island rotation. More reliably, the Limones Cays open the Day 2 island tour on every overnight cabin stay: the boat leaves the island around 7:45 AM for Limones and the shipwreck snorkel, then continues to the Natural Pool at about 8:30 AM before turning back toward Carti.

## Uninhabited means uninhabited

There is no community on these cays, which is the source of both their charm and their limitations. No shop, no drinking water, no toilets, no reliable shade beyond what the palms happen to throw. Everything you need for the stop travels with you in the boat, and everything you brought leaves with you — nothing is left behind on the sand, not even organic waste.

## Good to know

- **Best for:** photographers, and travellers who would rather see several distinct beaches than settle onto one.
- **Facilities:** none. Bring water, biodegradable sunscreen and a hat.
- **Comarca tax:** the Guna Yala entry tax is paid in cash on arrival in the territory, regardless of which cays your boat visits.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many cays do you actually visit at Limones?
It depends on the day's route and sea conditions, but the group is compact enough that two or three stops in one visit is normal rather than exceptional. The short hops between them are part of what the group is chosen for.

### Are the Limones Cays part of a day tour or an overnight stay?
Both. The VIP day tour includes this group in its island rotation, and every overnight cabin stay begins its Day 2 island tour here, at around 7:45 AM, before the Natural Pool.

### Is there anything on the cays — food, water, toilets?
No. These are uninhabited islets with no infrastructure of any kind. Lunch and drinking water come with the tour, and there are no facilities on the sand, so plan the stop as a beach and swim rather than a break with amenities.

### How do the Limones Cays differ from Cayos Holandeses?
Limones is close in, quick to reach and built around hopping between several small beaches. Cayos Holandeses is far out, reached on its own tour with an earlier departure and a minimum group size, and it is chosen for reef quality rather than for beaches. They are different days, not different prices for the same day.

### Does anyone live on the Limones Cays?
No permanent community lives on them, which is why they carry no community entry fee of their own. The Guna Yala comarca entry tax still applies to you as a visitor to the territory, and it is paid in cash at the border, not on the cays.
