Chichime is not one island but two: a pair of small cays lying side by side, separated by a channel shallow enough to wade across at low tide. That single quirk of geography is why it turns up in more San Blas photographs than almost any other stop — powder-white sand, a curtain of leaning palms, and water that shifts from pale turquoise to deep blue within a few meters of the shore.

## Two cays, one channel

The channel between the cays is the part people remember. At low tide it drops to knee or thigh height and you can cross on foot, sandbar to sandbar, with open Caribbean on either side of you. As the water rises the same crossing becomes a short swim instead. It also means the cay you land on and the cay in your photo may not be the same one — worth knowing before you leave a dry bag on the wrong beach.

## The Guna families who live here

A small Guna fishing community lives on one of the two cays, which makes Chichime an inhabited island rather than an empty postcard. Dugout canoes — *ulus* — sit pulled up on the sand beside nets drying in the sun, and they are working equipment, not scenery. The usual courtesies apply: ask before photographing people or homes, stay out of residential areas, and remember that any mola offered for sale was hand-stitched by the woman selling it.

## Snorkeling for complete beginners

The reef starts just off the shore and stays shallow, which is what a first-time snorkeler actually needs: no current to fight, no long swim out, and sand you can stand on if your mask floods. Expect sea fans, small reef fish and the occasional starfish in clear water. Guides ask for biodegradable sunscreen for a concrete reason — ordinary sunscreen chemicals damage the coral you came to see.

## Getting to Chichime

Chichime sits toward the western edge of the archipelago and is reached like every other island here: pickup in Panama City between 5:00 and 5:45 AM, roughly two and a half hours by 4x4 to the Carti embarkation point, then a speedboat out. Most Classic and VIP day-tour itineraries from Carti include a Chichime stop, usually paired with a second nearby cay so you can compare two very different islands in one day.

## Good to know

- **Best for:** first-time visitors, easy snorkeling, sunset and postcard photography.
- **Facilities:** basic — thatched shelters for shade, no running water, no electricity for visitors, no shop.
- **Cash:** the comarca entry tax is paid in cash on arrival in Guna Yala, and there are no ATMs anywhere in the territory.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you really walk between the two Chichime cays?
At low tide, yes — the channel separating them is shallow enough to wade across, which is how most visitors cross. Tide levels change through the day, so at other hours the same crossing is a short swim rather than a walk. Your guide will tell you which one it is on the day you visit.

### Is Chichime a good place to snorkel if I have never tried it?
It is one of the gentlest places in San Blas to start. The reef sits close to shore in shallow water, and you are never far from sand you can stand on. Life jackets are mandatory on the boats for adults and children alike, and nothing stops you from keeping yours on in the water.

### Do I have to pay anything when I arrive at Chichime?
Not to the island itself, but every visitor to Guna Yala pays a comarca entry tax — $22 for foreigners and $7 for residents with a valid passport or residency card — in cash, separately from the tour price. It goes directly to the Guna community. Bring the cash with you, because there are no ATMs in the comarca.

### Can I sleep on Chichime?
Overnight stays in San Blas are booked by cabin type rather than by a named island, and the island is confirmed closer to your date based on availability, so Chichime cannot be promised by name. Chichime appears instead as a stop on the day-tour and multi-day itineraries that depart from Carti.

### What should I bring for a day on Chichime?
Biodegradable sunscreen, a hat and sunglasses, a towel, a dry bag, your original passport or ID — a photocopy is not accepted at the comarca checkpoint — and enough cash for the entry tax. There is no shop on the cay and no drinking water for visitors, so whatever you need has to arrive with you on the boat.
