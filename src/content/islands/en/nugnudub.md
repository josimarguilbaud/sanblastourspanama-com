If the better-known San Blas islands feel crowded on a busy tour day, Nugnudub is the correction. It is small, less visited, and home to an active Guna community — the kind of stop that shows up on itineraries built around a slower pace rather than back-to-back beaches.

## What you will not find here

There is no shipwreck to snorkel, no famous sandbar, no photograph everyone already has. Nugnudub is not a "wow" island in the postcard sense, and pretending otherwise would set you up for disappointment. If a checklist of highlights is what you want from your day, this is not the stop to push for.

## What an inhabited island actually looks like

What it offers instead is unarranged. Homes, dugout canoes pulled up on the sand, nets, cooking, people going about a Tuesday — none of it set up for visitors, because the island is not primarily a tourism venue. That is the difference between visiting a community and visiting a display, and it is the whole reason to come here rather than to another beach.

## Molas, and buying one well

The intricate hand-stitched panels you will see for sale are molas, a genuine Guna textile tradition, and they are very often made by the women selling them. Buying directly from the maker is the version of this transaction that supports the artisan rather than a reseller. Ask about a piece before assuming a price; the work in one panel is measured in days, not minutes.

## Etiquette that actually matters

Ask before photographing people, homes or canoes — a friendly question changes the whole encounter, and the answer is usually yes. Some parts of an inhabited island are residential rather than open to wandering visitors, so stay where you are welcome. Expect a small community entry fee, paid in cash, separate from your tour price; the Guna Yala comarca entry tax that every visitor pays on arriving in the territory is a different payment, made at the border.

## Whether your tour stops here

Honestly: it may not. Nugnudub is not a guaranteed stop on any fixed itinerary — like every island in San Blas, whether a given boat calls here depends on the day's route, the weather and availability. It is worth naming as a preference when you book, and worth not building your expectations on.

## Good to know

- **Best for:** travellers who want a quieter, more human stop instead of another beach.
- **Community:** this is somebody's home, and the courtesies that apply to a village anywhere apply here.
- **Route note:** never a fixed stop; ask about it in advance rather than on the boat.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Nugnudub a fixed stop on any tour?
No. It is not part of any published fixed itinerary, and whether a boat visits depends on the day's route, sea conditions and availability. If seeing an inhabited, less-visited island matters to you, say so when you book so it can be taken into account.

### Can I photograph people on the island?
Ask first. Homes, canoes and daily routines are not a backdrop by default, and a direct, friendly question is both the courteous approach and usually the successful one. Photographs of the beach and the water need no permission; photographs of people do.

### What is a mola, and where should I buy one?
A mola is a hand-stitched textile panel and a genuine Guna art form, not a souvenir manufactured elsewhere. The best place to buy one is directly from the woman who made it, on an island like this one, where the money reaches the artisan rather than an intermediary.

### Is there a fee to visit an inhabited island?
Expect a small community entry fee per visitor, paid in cash on the island and separate from your tour price. That is distinct from the Guna Yala comarca entry tax, which every visitor pays in cash on entering the territory. There are no ATMs anywhere in Guna Yala, so bring enough cash for both.

### What is the difference between an inhabited island and an uninhabited cay?
For a visitor, mostly expectations. An uninhabited cay is sand, palms and water, with no facilities and nobody to consider. An inhabited island has residents, routines, boundaries and usually a community fee — you are a guest rather than the only person on the beach, and it is worth arriving with that in mind.
